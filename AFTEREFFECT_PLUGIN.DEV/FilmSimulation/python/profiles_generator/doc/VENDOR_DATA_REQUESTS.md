## 0. Glossary — what each requested parameter actually is

Written for you, not for the recipients. For each item: what it is, how it looks
on a datasheet, which part of the simulator it feeds, and what breaks without it.

Read the last column first if you are triaging. "Have it" means the simulator has
real sourced values for most stocks; "gap" means it mostly does not.

---

### The exposure-to-density relationship

**Characteristic curve** (also *D–logE curve*, *H&D curve*, *sensitometric
curve*). The single most important measurement. A graph: horizontal axis is the
logarithm of exposure, vertical axis is the resulting density (how dark the
developed film is). Colour films have three curves, one per layer.

It has three regions, and the names come up constantly:

- **Toe** — the shadow end, where the curve bends up slowly out of the base
  level. Its shape determines how shadows roll off.
- **Straight line** — the middle, where density rises proportionally to log
  exposure.
- **Shoulder** — the highlight end, where the curve flattens as the emulsion
  saturates.

*Simulator:* `ToneCurve` per channel. Modelled as a difference of two softplus
functions, which is why toe and shoulder curvature are separate parameters.
*Status:* **have it** for most tier-1 stocks. This is what makes a profile good.

**Gamma (γ)** — the slope of the straight-line section. High gamma = contrasty.
Colour negatives run about 0.55–0.65; print films run 2.5–3.5, which is how the
negative/print pair multiplies back to roughly 1.0 overall. Datasheets sometimes
give **contrast index** or **average gradient** instead — a slope measured
between two defined density points rather than a true tangent. Not identical, but
usable.

**Dmin** (also *base plus fog*, *b+f*) — density of the film with no exposure at
all. It is never zero: the support has its own colour, and some silver develops
unexposed. For a colour negative this is what makes the orange mask. Its
**per-channel values are the base tint**.
*Simulator:* `dmin` per channel. *Status:* **have it** mostly.

**Dmax** — the highest density the film can reach. Matters most for reversal
stocks, where it sets black depth.

**Latitude** — how many stops of over- and under-exposure still land on a usable
part of the curve. Not a separate measurement; it falls out of the curve shape.

---

### Colour and spectral behaviour

**Spectral sensitivity** — how sensitive each layer is at each wavelength, from
about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (deep red). Plotted as *log sensitivity* against
wavelength, normally with each layer's peak normalised to 0. Sensitivity itself
is defined as the reciprocal of the exposure needed to reach a stated density,
so a *higher* number means *less* light needed.

This is what makes a film render colours the way it does — how much the green
layer also responds to blue light, where the red layer's cut-off sits, and so on.
Requires a **monochromator** or a **wedge spectrograph** to measure. Cannot be
recovered from scans, ever.

*Simulator:* `SpectralSpec.log_s_b / log_s_g / log_s_r`.
*Status:* **gap** — only 35 of 93 stocks have real curves.

**Dye spectral densities** — once developed, each layer holds a dye (yellow,
magenta, cyan). This is how much each dye absorbs at each wavelength. Different
from spectral sensitivity: sensitivity is about *capturing* light, dye density
about *how the finished image transmits* it. Needed to model printing through to
a print stock, and to model what a scanner sees.
*Status:* **gap** for older stocks.

**Panchromatic / orthochromatic / isopanchromatic** — three spectral classes of
black-and-white film, not three grades of quality:

- **Orthochromatic** — sensitive to blue and green, effectively **blind to red**.
  Red lips render black, blue sky renders blank white. The signature look of
  pre-1930s cinema.
- **Panchromatic** — sensitive across the whole visible range.
- **Isopanchromatic** — a European term for panchromatic with a deliberately
  flattened, more even response. Use this word with Agfa and ORWO; they do.

**Balance / colour temperature** — which light source the film is designed for.
**T** = tungsten, 3200 K. **D** = daylight, 5500 K. A tungsten film shot in
daylight goes strongly blue, and vice versa. In catalogue numbers this appears as
"500T", "250D".

**Status M / Status A densitometry** — standardised filter sets for *measuring*
density. **Status M** is for camera negatives (it approximates what a print film
sees). **Status A** is for reversal films and prints (it approximates the eye).
Why you should care: **a curve measured on Status M and one measured on Status A
are not comparable.** Always note which one a datasheet used. If it does not say,
that is itself worth asking about.

---

### Grain

**RMS granularity** — a single number for how grainy the film is. Measured by
scanning a uniformly exposed patch with a small aperture, taking the standard
deviation of the density fluctuations, and multiplying by 1000. So "granularity
13" means σ(D) = 0.013.

**The measuring aperture must be stated, and this is why the letters insist on
it.** Kodak uses a **48 µm** circular aperture; a 24 µm aperture measures a
smaller area, sees bigger fluctuations, and returns a larger number for the very
same film. A granularity figure without its aperture is close to meaningless.
The density at which it was measured matters too — usually 1.0 above base.

*Simulator:* `GrainSpec.rms_r / rms_g / rms_b`.
*Status:* partly **gap**. Recoverable from scans — this is where scans genuinely
shine.

**Granularity vs graininess** — granularity is the *objective* measurement above.
**Graininess** is the *subjective* impression of a viewer. They correlate but are
not the same quantity; datasheets give granularity.

**Grain / clump size** — individual silver grains are far too small to see. What
you actually see is clumps, described in the classic literature as roughly six
grains piled over one another. Clump size drives the *texture scale* rather than
the amount.

---

### Sharpness

**MTF (modulation transfer function)** — how well the film reproduces fine
detail. A curve: horizontal axis is spatial frequency in **cycles per
millimetre**, vertical axis is the fraction of contrast surviving, normalised to
1.0 (or 100 %) at coarse detail. A film that still passes 50 % at 50 cycles/mm is
sharp; one that drops to 20 % is soft.

MTF can exceed 100 % at low frequencies on some films — a real effect from
development-inhibitor diffusion at edges (the **Eberhard effect**), not a
measurement error.

**Resolving power** — the older, cruder measure: the finest line pattern
resolvable, in **line pairs per millimetre (lp/mm)**, always quoted at a stated
test-object contrast (Kodak uses 1000:1 and 1.6:1 — the high-contrast number is
always the bigger one, so the contrast must be quoted with it).

*Simulator:* MTF drives the per-channel blur; `_RESOLVING_POWER` holds the lp/mm
pairs. *Status:* **gap** for many stocks, including all VISION3 — though the
Kodak H-1 sheets normally print MTF, so check before asking.

---

### Time and storage

**Dark fade / dye stability** — colour dyes decay in storage even in complete
darkness. Expressed as dye density lost after so many years at a stated
temperature and relative humidity. **Light fade** is the separate, faster decay
under projection or display.

This is why old colour film looks wrong today: the three dyes fade at *different*
rates, so the image shifts hue overall, not just weaker. Cyan usually goes first,
leaving the familiar red-pink Eastmancolor prints.

*Simulator:* `AgingSpec`. *Status:* **empty for all 93 stocks.** No consumer
datasheet ever carried it. This is the single biggest hole in the project, which
is why every letter asks.

**Reciprocity failure** — the assumption that exposure = intensity × time breaks
down at very long or very short exposures; the film becomes less sensitive than
arithmetic predicts. Classically modelled as I·tᵖ (the *Schwarzschild* relation),
though p is not actually constant, and failure occurs on *both* sides of an
optimal intensity.
*Simulator:* `ReciprocitySpec`. *Status:* mostly **gap**.

---

### Colour-film internals

**Interimage effect (IIE)** — in modern colour negatives, each layer releases a
development inhibitor that suppresses its neighbours (**DIR couplers** —
Development Inhibitor Releasing). The effect steepens each channel's effective
gamma and increases colour separation. It is the main reason a 1990s negative
looks more saturated than a 1950s one at the same contrast.
*Simulator:* `InterimageSpec`, calibrated from patent data.

**Halation** — light passes through the emulsion, reflects off the base, and
comes back to expose the emulsion a second time slightly off-axis, producing a
glow around bright highlights. Anti-halation layers reduce it but never remove it.
*Status:* recoverable from scans, and we do.

**Monopack / integral tripack** — three emulsion layers coated on one strip of
film, as opposed to the older methods of running three separate films
(Technicolor) or one film with a colour mosaic. Agfacolor-Neu in 1936 was the
first practical one.

**Réseau** — the fine mosaic of coloured lines or patches on additive films like
Dufaycolor. Specified in **lines per millimetre**. Dufaycolor's was 19–25 lp/mm.

---

### Speed ratings, and how to convert them

Old European and Soviet documents use scales that are not ISO:

| Scale | Note |
|---|---|
| **ASA** / **ISO** (arithmetic) | Doubling the number = one stop faster. ISO 100 = ASA 100 |
| **DIN** (degrees) | Logarithmic. **+3 DIN = twice as fast.** 21 DIN = ISO 100 |
| **GOST** (Soviet) | Roughly 0.9 × ASA |

Conversion for DIN: `ASA ≈ 100 × 2^((DIN − 21) / 3)`.
Check: 15 DIN → 25 ASA, 18 DIN → 50 ASA. Both match published pairs.

Cross-check from a document we hold — the ORWO UT 18 leaflet states
**18 DIN / 50 ASA / 45 GOST** on one line, which confirms both relations at once.

German sheets often write DIN as a fraction, e.g. **"15/10 DIN"** — that is 15
DIN; the /10 is part of the historical notation, not a division.

**EI (Exposure Index)** — the speed the manufacturer recommends in practice,
which may differ slightly from the measured ISO speed.

---

### Process names

Not parameters, but they identify *which* emulsion generation you are being sent:

- **ECN / ECN-2** — Eastman Colour Negative process. ECN-2 arrived in 1974;
  anything earlier used the original ECN, so a 1959 stock is not ECN-2.
- **ECP / ECP-2** — the matching print process.
- **E-6** — the standard colour reversal process.
- **Kodachrome process (K-14 and predecessors)** — Kodachrome has *no* couplers
  in the emulsion; the dyes are formed in the machine. This is why it is
  exceptionally sharp and has no interimage effects.
- **C-41** — the standard still colour negative process.

---

### Things that are worth more than a datasheet

**Step wedge / step tablet** — a strip of film exposed through a calibrated
series of steps, typically 21 steps of 0.15 log exposure each. Because the
exposure at every step is *known*, densitometer readings from it reconstruct the
entire characteristic curve directly.

**Control strip** — a pre-exposed step wedge that a lab processes alongside
production work to check the process is in spec. Archives may still hold them,
and they are gold for the same reason: **known exposure**.

This is the crux of §2. Scanning ordinary film gives you `film ⊗ scene ⊗
exposure ⊗ processing ⊗ age ⊗ scanner` with the scene and exposure unknown, so it
cannot be untangled. A step wedge removes that unknown, which is why one wedge is
worth more than a thousand frames.

### Priority C — dark fade: ASK THE ARCHIVES, NOT THE MANUFACTURERS

Position changed on 2026-08-04, after the data turned up in a book already on
this machine. Read this before writing anything about dye fade.

**Much of it is already in hand.** Wilhelm & Brower, *The Permanence and Care
of Color Photographs* (1993), Chapter 5, carries Arrhenius accelerated
dark-fading tables. Six stocks are now populated in `FilmActiveProfiles.md`
from it: Kodachrome 64 at 580 years, Ektachrome 160T at 11-20, Ektachrome 64 at
6-10, Konica Impresa 50 at 20, Eastman 5254 and 5247 at under 6 years, plus
Polaroid SX-70 stain figures. Local copy:
`PDF/PROFILES/The Permanence and Care of Color Photographs.pdf`.

**Do NOT put this question to Kodak or to Agfa-Gevaert.** Three reasons:

1. It is not neutral the way sensitometry is. Curves and granularity were
   published sales material; dye stability is a liability and reputation
   matter. Kodak's fading Eastmancolor negatives drew a public campaign in the
   1980s and real legal exposure.
2. There is documented precedent for refusal. The footnote to Wilhelm's Table
   5.14 states plainly: *"Kodak has declined to release dye-stability data for
   these films."* An established researcher asked and was refused.
3. The real risk is not a "no" but CONTAMINATING THE REST OF THE LETTER. One
   legally sensitive question among a dozen innocuous ones can route the whole
   request to legal review, and legal review means no reply at all. That trades
   a request that would have been answered for one that will not be.

Marginal value is low in any case: Wilhelm compiled his tables FROM
manufacturer data — "published Kodak data", "data supplied by Konica", the Agfa
and Fuji tables. Asking the vendor mostly asks for a source already public.

**Do ask the archives and the institutes,** where there is no liability
exposure and image stability is the professional subject. Reframe it, though:
request **ISO 18909 (formerly ISO 10977) test reports, or Arrhenius dark-fading
data**, rather than asking how quickly something faded. A standardised test
report is a neutral archival request; "how fast did it fade" implies a defect.

**Still genuinely missing, and only partly obtainable:**
- per-dye rates. Wilhelm publishes only the LEAST STABLE dye plus its identity,
  so two of three rates remain unknown for every stock.
- activation energies, without which only one storage condition can be modelled.
- light-fade data, where the ranking INVERTS: Kodachrome is the most stable film
  in dark storage and among the least stable under projection.
- anything at all for ORWO, SVEMA, TASMA, Gevacolor camera stocks or Dufaycolor.

**One question that IS worth putting to the manufacturers,** because only they
can answer it and it carries no sensitivity: **archived sensitometric
control-strip readings** for discontinued stock. A control strip is a known
exposure, which is exactly what cannot be recovered from scanning finished film.

## 1a. Official product designations — use these exact names

Purpose of this table: name the **product instance**, not the family, so nobody
at the other end has to guess. "Ektachrome" alone names about forty different
emulsions across fifty years; "KODAK EKTACHROME 64 Professional Film (EPR),
Kodak publication E-8" names exactly one.

**Confidence is marked, and it matters.** ✅ = the designation is taken from a
manufacturer document we hold and cite. ⚠️ = the film is real and correctly
identified, but the *exact registered wording and catalogue number are not
confirmed from a primary source* — for those, ask by description plus year and
let the archivist supply the official name. Do not present a ⚠️ row as if it
were the registered title.

Two conventions worth stating in the letter itself, because they remove most
ambiguity:

- **Kodak cine catalogue numbers encode the gauge.** `5xxx` = 35 mm,
  `7xxx` = 16 mm (and Super 8 in some lines). The emulsion is the same; the
  number differs. So 5222 and 7222 are one emulsion on two gauges — worth saying
  explicitly, since a request for "5222" may be read as excluding the 16 mm data.
- **Balance suffixes:** `T` = tungsten 3200 K, `D` = daylight 5500 K.

## 2. Can film frame scans replace a missing datasheet?

**Partly — and the split is sharp, not gradual.** Worth understanding before you
spend weeks scanning.

The reason is simple: a finished frame is

    scan  =  film ⊗ scene ⊗ exposure ⊗ processing ⊗ printing ⊗ age ⊗ scanner

With the scene and exposure unknown, that cannot be inverted. Anything defined
relative to a *known input* is unrecoverable; anything that is a *statistical or
structural property of the emulsion* survives.

### Recoverable from ordinary frame scans — genuinely good

- **Grain**: RMS-like sigma, clump size, per-channel ratios, and how all three
  vary between shadow, mid-tone and highlight. Already done this way for
  SVEMA FN-64 (355 frames) and ORWO UT18 (78 frames) — those are among the
  best-grounded grain models in the database.
- **Halation**: radius and strength, from ring profiles around specular
  highlights.
- **Base density and base tint** — *but only if the unexposed rebate or frame
  edge is inside the scan.* This is the single cheapest thing you can do to
  improve a scan's value.
- **Dye crossover** and the current **aged dye state**.
- **Coating and edge defects**, uneven emulsion at frame edges, réseau pitch on
  mosaic stocks.

### NOT recoverable from ordinary frame scans

- **Characteristic curve / gamma** — needs a known exposure.
- **ISO speed** — same.
- **Spectral sensitivity** — needs a monochromator or wedge spectrograph.
- **MTF / resolving power** — needs a known test target at known contrast.
- **Reciprocity behaviour**, **interimage coefficients**.

Claiming otherwise would be inventing numbers, which is how the Agfacolor
Type B red layer ended up 30 nm off before it was caught.

### One caveat that matters for old stock

A scan of 60-year-old film measures the **aged** emulsion, not the fresh one.
That is not a defect — it is often what you actually want to render — but it
must be labelled. ORWO UT18 is stored in the database explicitly as an aged
state, with `balance_kelvin` documented as modelling the aged dyes rather than
the factory daylight rating.

### What turns a scan into something nearly as good as a datasheet

If you ever have access to the stock or to archive material, these are worth
far more than more frames:

1. **A sensitometric step wedge exposed on that stock** (21-step, known logE).
   This alone yields the entire characteristic curve — the single biggest gap.
2. **The unexposed rebate in the same scan** — free dmin and base tint.
3. **A grey card or colour chart in the original photographed frame.**
4. **A characterised scanner**: scan a calibrated transmission target with
   identical settings, so the scanner's own transfer can be divided out.
5. **Stated processing and storage history** — original process or modern
   substitute (ECN-2 vs the original), and how the film was kept.

Items 1 and 2 are the ones that change the answer categorically. More frames of
unknown scenes mostly improve grain statistics that are already converged.

## 4. Before you send — read this once

**Expect reproduction fees from the archives, and ask about them upfront.**
This matters given your standing rule about not incurring costs. Manufacturers
usually send a PDF for free if they have one. **Museums and university special
collections normally charge** for scanning, staff research time, and sometimes
for permission to use material — anywhere from a nominal per-page rate to
substantial commercial-use licensing. Every archive draft below therefore asks
for a cost estimate *before* any work is done, so nothing can be billed to you
by surprise. Do not drop that sentence when you edit.

**State plainly that the project is non-commercial — it changes the price.**
Most archives operate two tariffs, and the gap is large: private, academic and
non-commercial research is commonly charged at a reduced rate or waived
entirely, while commercial licensing is charged properly. Saying so in the first
message means you are quoted the right rate from the start instead of having to
negotiate down from the commercial one. Every draft below says it explicitly.

modest amount: a few euros or dollars for a scan is fine. **EUR 100 for a single

whether a lower-resolution scan, a photograph of the relevant page, or simply
*densitometer readings transcribed by staff* would be cheaper — for our purposes
the numbers matter, not the image quality. Reading-room self-service photography
is often free or nearly so, which is another fallback worth asking about.

Consultation of a document is one thing; *republishing* it is another. You want

## 5. Register and etiquette  *(internal note, do not paste into letters)*

All seven letters below are written for **first contact with an institution
where no individual is known by name**, in a formal register with no
familiarity. Points that are easy to get wrong:

- **"Dear Sir or Madam," pairs with "Yours faithfully,"**. If you learn a
  person's name, change the opening to "Dear Ms Smith," and the closing to
  "Yours sincerely,". Mixing the pairs reads as carelessness to anyone who
  notices, and archivists notice.
- **No contractions.** "I am writing" rather than "I'm writing", "would not"
  rather than "wouldn't". This is the single largest difference in tone.
- **No enthusiasm markers.** No exclamation marks, nothing described as
  "exciting" or "amazing". Understatement is the register: "of considerable
  value", not "invaluable".
- **State your standing plainly and without inflation.** You are an independent
  developer working on a non-commercial project. Say exactly that. Do not
  imply an institutional affiliation you do not hold — it is easily checked and
  it would end the correspondence.
- **Use the private address, never the work one.** All correspondence below is
  signed `maratshch@gmail.com`. This is deliberate and applies to every letter
  in this file and to any future one: the project is personal and
  non-commercial, so a corporate address would misrepresent it, invite the
  assumption that an employer is behind the request, and risk the recipient
  applying a commercial rate. Keep the two separate.
- **One page.** Attach nothing on first contact.
- **Do not apologise more than once.** A single sentence acknowledging that the
  request is unusual is courteous; repeated apology reads as insecurity.
- Address to "Dear Sir or Madam" only when the institution publishes no named
  contact. Check the website first; a named archivist raises your odds
  considerably.

The honest odds are better with the archives than with the manufacturers, with
two exceptions: ILFORD and FILM Ferrania are both still making film and both
have a culture of talking to enthusiasts, which makes them better prospects than
their size suggests.

1. **ILFORD / HARMAN technology** — still manufacturing, technically
   communicative, and the request is narrow and specific. The one caveat is that
   granularity is a deliberate non-disclosure rather than an oversight, so the

3. **Image Permanence Institute** — narrow, expert, exactly their subject. Most
   likely of all to answer knowledgeably even if the answer is a reference

8. **George Eastman Museum** — strong holdings; the reference-strip question

   Check the published archive pages first; the letter may prove unnecessary.
10. **Kodak** — low odds on the historical sheets, but the only possible source

Three points of discipline, repeated because each has already caught us once.

**Download before asking.** Check <https://125px.com/techdocs/kodak/>, Ilford's
own technical sheets, and Fujifilm's cine archive pages before sending anything.
Asking six institutions for a document that is already public would be
embarrassing, and it spends goodwill that cannot be recovered.

**Do not put the dye-fade question to Kodak or Agfa-Gevaert.** See §1 Priority C.
One legally sensitive question can cost you the reply to the other twelve.

**Verify every address on the institution's own site.** Three of the eleven
could not be verified here, and a bounced first contact cannot be retried
gracefully.
